Study Session 1: Communication and Language Skills

Meaning, importance, definitions, Seven C’s, types, elements and language skills.

Short-answer questions

Answer: Communication is the sharing or exchange of ideas, feelings, thoughts, information, messages and meaning with others. It sustains society and enables people to express emotions and needs, share information, understand one another, cooperate and build relationships. Without communication, society would experience disorder and chaos.

Answer: Ojomo (2004:78) defines communication as the sharing of ideas, feelings, thoughts, information and messages with others. Rothwell (2001) describes it as a transactional process of sharing meaning with others.

Answer: Communication involves sharing symbolic meanings and increasing commonality among participants. It is a dynamic exchange and interaction through which meaning changes and is shared. It can involve a source transmitting a message to a receiver with the conscious intention of affecting behaviour. Communication also affects other people, our physical environment and ourselves.

Answer:

  1. Clarity: make the message easy to understand.
  2. Correctness: use accurate language, grammar and information.
  3. Courtesy: be polite and respectful.
  4. Concreteness: use specific, precise details.
  5. Conciseness: avoid unnecessary words and repetition.
  6. Completeness: include all necessary information.
  7. Consideration: take the receiver’s needs and perspective into account.

Answer: Verbal communication uses spoken words/oral language. Non-verbal communication occurs without speech and may involve gestures and body signs; the course also classifies written texts as non-verbal because they do not use spoken expressions. Intra-personal communication occurs within an individual, such as thought or monologue. Inter-personal communication is the exchange of information between individuals.

Answer: Formal communication occurs in formal settings and often follows organisational hierarchy. It generally avoids colloquial expressions, slang and abbreviations. Informal communication occurs in casual settings and commonly permits colloquialism, slang and abbreviations.

  1. Sender: source/person who initiates the communication.
  2. Message: information, idea or content being communicated.
  3. Channel: medium through which the message is sent, such as oral or written.
  4. Receiver/recipient: person who receives the message.
  5. Feedback: receiver’s response to the message.

Answer: Listening, Speaking, Reading and Writing. Listening and Reading are receptive skills because they involve receiving/understanding information. Speaking and Writing are expressive skills because they involve expressing or producing messages.

Essay questions

Model answer: Communication is a daily activity through which people share or exchange ideas, feelings, thoughts, information, messages and meaning. It is essential to the survival and orderly functioning of society. It allows individuals to express emotions and needs, inform others, cooperate, develop relationships and understand one another. In its absence, chaos and disorder would result.

Ojomo (2004:78) emphasises sharing ideas, feelings, thoughts, information and messages with others. Rothwell (2001) views communication as a transactional process of sharing meaning. The Macbride Commission Report, as cited by Aina (2003), highlights symbolic meaning, commonality, dynamic exchange, interaction, intentional influence on behaviour, and effects on others, the environment and ourselves. The definitions share the ideas of sharing, exchanging, meaning, interaction and transmitting information. They show communication as a process involving people and meaning, rather than simply sending words.

Model answer: Verbal/oral communication uses spoken words, for example a lecture or conversation. Non-verbal communication does not use speech and may involve gestures or body signs; written texts are also treated as non-verbal in this course. Intra-personal communication occurs within a person, such as silently considering a decision. Inter-personal communication occurs between individuals, such as two students discussing an assignment.

Formal communication is used in official situations and often follows hierarchy, with language that avoids slang and abbreviations. Informal communication is casual and may use colloquialism, slang and abbreviations. The course also mentions business, organisational and cultural communication, each of which has its own context, style and discourse.

Model answer: A sender initiates communication by preparing a message. The message is transmitted through a channel, such as oral speech or writing, to a receiver. The receiver interprets it and provides feedback, which is the response. Feedback helps the sender know whether the message was received or understood and may allow clarification or correction. The process involves sender, message, channel, receiver and feedback.

Study Session 2: Understanding Writing — Meaning and Types

Meaning and purpose of writing, functional and purpose-based types, audience, and writing skills.

Short-answer questions

Answer: Writing is an expressive language and communication skill through which writers express thoughts, feelings, ideas, opinions, attitudes and purposes. It enables people to share information, persuade or convince others, communicate with known or unknown audiences, write personal notes, and provide enjoyment.

Answer: Audience and purpose determine the content, language, tone, style, level of detail and organisation appropriate for a piece of writing. Writing to inform a lecturer differs from writing a personal note or entertaining readers.

  • Narrative: tells a story or recounts events, often in sequence and past tense.
  • Descriptive: creates a vivid picture of a person, place, object or experience using details and sensory impressions.
  • Persuasive: seeks to convince readers through reasons, arguments or evidence.
  • Expository: explains or discusses a subject or topical issue; it may include facts and sometimes opinions.

Answer: Creative writing includes poetry, drama and prose and uses imagination, inspiration and practice. Academic writing is produced for educational or scholarly purposes, follows prescribed formats and requires references; plagiarism must be avoided. Journalistic writing is produced for the press, follows an in-house style and may be expository; references are not compulsory in the same way as academic writing.

Answer: Plagiarism is academic theft: presenting another person’s words or ideas as one’s own without appropriate acknowledgement. It is unacceptable because academic writing requires honesty and acknowledgement of sources through referencing.

  • Linguistic skills: knowledge and use of language, grammar, punctuation, capitalization, spelling, lexical choice, collocation and dictionary skills.
  • Compositional skills: planning and developing a text, considering audience/content, drafting and reviewing/revising/editing.
  • Presentational skills: arranging the text clearly through paragraphing, coherence, unity, completeness, clarity and avoidance of ambiguity.

Answer: The process involves planning; thinking about content and audience; drafting; and reviewing, revising and editing. Writing is a process, and writers may return to earlier stages as they improve their work.

Answer: “Slow, men are working on the road.” uses the comma to address or warn people to slow down because men are working. “Slow men are working on the road.” describes the men as slow. The punctuation changes the interpretation, showing why punctuation is important for meaning.

Essay questions

Model answer: Writing is an expressive communication skill that enables a person to put ideas, thoughts, feelings, opinions and attitudes into written form. It may be used to share information, persuade, convince, entertain, express oneself or communicate with audiences who may be known or unknown. Writers may write for themselves, as in notes, or for other people, including readers of public writing.

Functional types include narrative, which recounts events; descriptive, which creates a vivid picture; persuasive, which seeks to convince; and expository, which explains or discusses a topic. Purpose-based types include creative writing (poetry, drama and prose), academic writing (scholarly or educational writing with prescribed formats and compulsory references) and journalistic writing (press writing guided by an in-house style). The writer should select a suitable type based on audience and purpose.

Model answer: Linguistic skills help a writer use correct and appropriate language, including grammar, punctuation, capitalization, spelling, vocabulary, lexical choice and collocation. Compositional skills concern the process of developing a text: planning, considering content and audience, drafting and reviewing/revising/editing. Presentational skills make the finished text readable and organised through paragraphing, unity, coherence, completeness and clarity, while avoiding ambiguity. Effective writing requires all three: language accuracy, thoughtful development and clear presentation.

Model answer: Academic writing is undertaken for educational or scholarly purposes, often through assignments, term papers or seminal papers. It follows prescribed formats and requires references. Journalistic writing is written for the press and follows the publication’s in-house style; it may include expository writing, and references are not compulsory in the same manner stated for academic work. Referencing in academic writing identifies sources, acknowledges others’ contributions and helps prevent plagiarism, which is academic theft.

Study Session 3: Essay Writing Skills

Essay types, writing stages, structure and common errors.

Short-answer questions
  • Narrative: recounts an experience or story, usually using past tense.
  • Argumentative: presents logical facts and ideas to persuade or convince; tense depends on context.
  • Descriptive: gives vivid details to create a mental picture, often using simple present for enduring attributes and sensory details.
  • Expository: discusses or explains a topical issue, often using research or facts; it may also contain subjective views.

Answer: Pre-writing is preparation before drafting. It includes choosing or understanding the topic, identifying purpose and audience, gathering information or points, and arranging ideas in an outline.

Answer: The writer develops the outline into an essay, builds paragraphs, explains and supports points, maintains organisation, and avoids plagiarism and needless repetition.

Answer: Post-writing involves reviewing the essay as a reader or critic, checking content and organisation, revising, editing and correcting errors. The writer should “cross the t’s and dot the i’s.”

Answer: The introduction usually consists of one paragraph and introduces the topic; in a narrative, it may capture the reader’s interest. The body contains paragraphs that develop points identified in the outline. The conclusion is usually one paragraph that gives an overview of the discussion without introducing new points.

  • Grammatical errors: problems such as tense, concord, spelling and capitalization.
  • Mechanical errors: incorrect punctuation.
  • Structural errors: poor sentence construction, paragraph arrangement, sentence variety, topic sentences, repetition or collocational problems.

Answer: An outline organises the main points before writing. It helps the writer maintain a logical sequence, ensure the body paragraphs correspond to the planned points, reduce omission and repetition, and keep the essay focused on its purpose.

Answer: Narrative essays commonly use past tense because they recount events or experiences that happened. Descriptive essays often use simple present when describing enduring attributes, helping present details as characteristics of the subject. Tense should still fit the context.

Essay questions

Model answer: Essay writing has three stages: pre-writing, writing and post-writing. At pre-writing, the writer identifies the topic, audience and purpose, gathers relevant information or ideas, and prepares an outline. This provides direction and organisation. During writing, the writer develops the outline into an introduction, body and conclusion, builds paragraphs around points, supports ideas and avoids plagiarism or repetition. At post-writing, the writer reviews the work critically, checks whether it communicates effectively, revises the organisation and wording, and edits grammatical, mechanical and structural errors. The stages improve the essay from planning to final presentation.

Model answer: A good essay has an introduction, body and conclusion. The introduction normally consists of one paragraph and introduces the subject or prepares the reader for the discussion; it may use an engaging opening, especially in narrative writing. The body consists of paragraphs developed from the points in the outline. Each paragraph should explain a point with relevant details and maintain unity and coherence. The conclusion usually consists of one paragraph that brings the discussion together and gives an overview. It should not introduce a new point. This structure makes the essay orderly and complete.

Model answer: Grammatical errors include wrong tense, spelling, capitalization and concord. Mechanical errors involve punctuation. Structural errors include faulty sentence construction, poor paragraph arrangement, lack of sentence variety, weak or missing topic sentences, repetition and collocational errors. Writers can reduce these errors by planning and outlining, using appropriate language resources such as dictionaries, checking grammar and punctuation, organising paragraphs logically, varying sentence structures, reviewing their writing and editing carefully after drafting.

Study Session 4: Paragraphs

Meaning, topic sentence, unity, coherence, emphasis and completeness.

Short-answer questions

Answer: A paragraph is a unit of thought and discourse. It discusses an idea independently, but it also relates to other paragraphs to contribute to the overall meaning of an essay.

Answer: A topic sentence identifies or underlines the significant message or central idea of a paragraph. It guides the reader and helps the writer keep the paragraph focused.

Answer: Unity means that all sentences in a paragraph relate to one central theme or point. The writer avoids digression and unrelated information.

Answer: Coherence means that ideas are logically arranged, meaningful, orderly and understandable. It can be achieved through logical sequencing, conjunctions and other cohesive links, appropriate use of pronouns, and avoiding ambiguity and unnecessary repetition.

Answer: Emphasis gives prominence to an idea according to its significance. It may be achieved through careful logical repetition or paraphrasing and through contrasting sentences or ideas.

Answer: Completeness means fully discussing the point raised in a paragraph. The writer should finish developing one point before moving to another paragraph, rather than leaving the discussion unfinished or spilling it into the next paragraph.

Answer: Unity concerns whether all sentences relate to one central idea and avoid digression. Coherence concerns whether the ideas are logically arranged and connected so that the paragraph is meaningful and easy to follow. A paragraph may have one topic but still be incoherent if its ideas are poorly ordered.

Answer: If an essay discusses the advantages and disadvantages of a subject, the writer can discuss the advantages fully in one paragraph and the disadvantages fully in another. This keeps each paragraph’s point complete and clearly organised.

Essay questions

Model answer: A good paragraph has unity, coherence, emphasis and completeness. Unity requires all sentences to focus on one central theme without digression. Coherence requires the ideas to be meaningful and logically arranged; conjunctions, pronouns and other cohesive devices help link sentences and reduce ambiguity. Emphasis gives prominence to the most significant idea and can be achieved through logical repetition, paraphrasing or contrast. Completeness requires the writer to fully discuss the point within the paragraph rather than leaving it unfinished or continuing it in the next paragraph. Together, these features make a paragraph focused, organised and effective.

Model answer: A topic sentence identifies the central or significant message of a paragraph and guides the development of the discussion. The supporting sentences should explain, illustrate or develop that point. Unity ensures they remain relevant, coherence arranges them logically and links them clearly, emphasis highlights the important message, and completeness ensures the point is fully developed. Paragraphs also connect with one another to build the overall essay. Proper organisation makes the essay easier to understand and prevents unrelated or incomplete discussion.

Study Session 5: Sentence Construction

Structural and functional sentence types; subject, verb, object, complement and adjunct.

Short-answer questions
  • Simple: has a subject and predicate; the predicate contains a predicator/verb.
  • Complex: has a main clause and a subordinate clause.
  • Compound: has at least two main/independent clauses joined by a coordinating conjunction such as and or but.
  • Compound-complex: has at least two main clauses and at least one subordinate clause.

Answer: A main clause can stand independently as a complete idea. A subordinate clause depends on a main clause and does not function as an independent complete sentence in that context. Complex sentences combine the two, often using a subordinating conjunction such as because.

  • Declarative: makes a statement; may be affirmative or negative.
  • Interrogative: asks a question; includes polar (yes/no) and non-polar (word, phrase or sentence response) questions.
  • Imperative: gives an order or makes a request, including polite requests.
  • Exclamatory: expresses emotion and commonly ends with an exclamation mark.

Answer: A polar question normally requires a yes/no answer, for example, “Are you coming to class tomorrow?” A non-polar question requires a word, phrase or sentence response, for example, “What is your name?”

  • Subject: the actor/topic, often before the verb.
  • Verb/predicator: the compulsory nucleus of the sentence; expresses action or links the subject to information.
  • Object: receives the action and often follows the verb.
  • Complement: completes or identifies the subject or meaning.
  • Adjunct: adverbial information such as time, place or reason.

Answer: The verb/predicator is compulsory in the sentence structure discussed in the course. It expresses an action or links the subject with other information and forms the core around which the predicate is organised.

Answer: Olu = subject; bought = verb/predicator; a book = object (the thing receiving the action of buying).

Answer: In “Olu bought a book,” a book is the object because it receives the action. In “The man is a teacher,” a teacher is the complement because it identifies/completes the subject. In “He slept soundly,” soundly is the adjunct because it adds adverbial information about how he slept.

Essay questions

Model answer: A simple sentence has a subject and predicate, with a verb/predicator in the predicate. Example: “Olu bought a new car.” A complex sentence contains a main clause and a subordinate clause, as in “She travelled to Lagos because she wanted to see her father.” The main clause is “She travelled to Lagos,” while the subordinate clause is “because she wanted to see her father.” A compound sentence contains at least two independent/main clauses joined by a coordinating conjunction: “I came for the lecture but the speaker did not come.” A compound-complex sentence combines both structures, containing at least two main clauses and one subordinate clause, for example: “She travelled to Lagos because she wanted to see her father but she did not meet him at home.”

Model answer: Declarative sentences make statements and may be affirmative or negative. An affirmative declarative does not contain “no” or “not” in the examples given, while a negative declarative contains a negating form, e.g., “Lagos is not the capital of Nigeria.” Interrogative sentences ask questions. Polar interrogatives generally take yes/no answers, while non-polar interrogatives take a word, phrase or sentence response. Imperatives give orders or make requests, including polite requests such as “Would you please close the door?” Exclamatory sentences express emotion and usually end with an exclamation mark. These functional categories describe what a sentence does.

Model answer: The subject is the actor or topic; the verb/predicator is the compulsory nucleus; the object receives the action; the complement completes or identifies the subject; and the adjunct adds adverbial details such as time, place or reason. In “Olu bought a book,” Olu is subject, bought is verb and a book is object. In “He slept soundly,” He is subject, slept is verb and soundly is adjunct. In “The man is a teacher,” The man is subject, is is verb and a teacher is complement. Understanding these elements helps construct clear and varied sentences.

Study Session 6: Comprehension

Meaning of comprehension and linguistic and psychological factors affecting understanding.

Short-answer questions

Answer: Comprehension means understanding. It is the ability to understand what is heard or read.

Answer: The purpose is to understand the information or meaning communicated. Reading aims at understanding what a text discusses; listening aims at understanding what is said.

Answer: Linguistic factors and psychological factors.

Answer: Linguistic factors concern the language of the text and the reader’s vocabulary range. Complex language or unfamiliar vocabulary may make a text difficult to understand. A reader’s vocabulary determines how much of the text’s meaning they can access.

Answer: Psychological factors concern the reader’s state of mind and emotional condition. Anxiety, emotional instability or even strong joy may distract a reader and affect focus, thereby reducing comprehension.

Answer: A wide vocabulary helps the reader understand more words and expressions in a passage. A limited vocabulary may cause difficulty understanding key terms, which affects the overall meaning of the text.

Answer: Anxiety, emotional instability, joy or other emotional conditions may interfere with concentration and focus, and therefore influence understanding.

Answer: Students must understand texts, instructions and learning materials to acquire knowledge and perform academic tasks. Reading without understanding does not fulfil the purpose of reading; comprehension is therefore central to learning and success.

Essay questions

Model answer: Comprehension means understanding. Reading and listening are undertaken to obtain meaning from written or spoken information. When a person reads, the aim is to understand what the text discusses; when a person listens, the aim is to understand what is communicated. Comprehension is important in education because students must understand course materials, instructions and information in order to learn. Merely reading or hearing words without understanding does not achieve the purpose of communication or study. Students’ success therefore depends substantially on their ability to comprehend texts.

Model answer: Linguistic factors relate to the language used in a text and the reader’s vocabulary range. A passage containing complex language or unfamiliar vocabulary may be difficult for a reader to understand. The more words and expressions the reader knows, the more meaning they can obtain from the passage. Psychological factors relate to the reader’s emotional condition and state of mind. Anxiety, emotional instability or joy may distract attention and reduce focus. Both the language of the text and the reader’s psychological state can therefore affect comprehension.

Model answer: Linguistic factors arise from language-related difficulties, such as complex vocabulary, unfamiliar expressions or a reader’s limited vocabulary. For example, a student may struggle with a passage because several words are unfamiliar. Psychological factors arise from the reader’s emotional state or mental focus. For example, an anxious or emotionally unsettled student may find it difficult to concentrate even when the passage uses familiar language. Linguistic factors concern the text and language knowledge; psychological factors concern the reader’s state of mind. Both can reduce understanding.
